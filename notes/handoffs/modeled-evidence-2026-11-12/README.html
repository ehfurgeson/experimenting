Handoff: grow both evidence pathways, then consider a combined pathway

Prepared 12 November 2026. This is a plan for a new agent, not a record of new acquisition, fitted toxicity models or a completed combined analysis.

1. The assignment in plain language

The professor wants comparisons supported by two kinds of evidence:

  1. Experimental pathway A: toxicity or plant responses measured in real experiments.
  2. External-model pathway B: estimates produced by documented outside models, such as EPA TEST. These estimates need not have a measurement for the compound.
  3. Possible combined pathway C, later: consider using both kinds together, after A and B have been developed, evaluated and reported separately.

The immediate priority is gathering as much useful, traceable data as possible from as many accessible sources as possible. Expand both the number of compounds and the number of sources. Keep the original records, including failed queries, out-of-domain predictions, missing results and disagreements. A larger number of rows is not automatically a larger number of independent compounds or studies.

Follow this order: audit → gather broadly → freeze separate datasets → model A and B separately → compare them → decide whether C is justified. Do not begin by mixing predictions into the existing measured tables. Do not start pathway C as a shortcut around a sparse experimental dataset.

This document was requested while the broader autonomous research goal was paused. Creating the handoff does not resume that goal. The next agent should follow the user's current authorization when starting execution.

4. Definitions that must stay separate

Term Meaning here What it does not establish
Natural occurrence A molecule is documented in a biological source Plant inactivity, efficacy or safety
Plant activity A response under a stated species/dose/time/endpoint Universal herbicidal activity
Experimental toxicity Measured outcome with traceable study context Comparability with every same-named endpoint
External estimate A named outside model's output A new experiment or certain biological truth
Our surrogate model Our model fitted to external estimates Independent experimental validation
Binding structure A deposited protein/ligand complex A quantitative binding affinity or field efficacy

A/B are evidence pathways, not replacements for Track1/Track2. Track1 studies non-target toxicity and group differences. Track2 studies natural herbicidal chemistry and plant activity. Either track may use A and B where scientifically appropriate. TEST non-target toxicity predictions do not supply plant-activity labels. For Track2, measured plant efficacy can be discussed alongside a separate external non-target-hazard analysis. If a documented, fit-for-purpose external plant-activity model cannot be found, record that gap; do not invent one.

Define “experimental-only” explicitly. At minimum all outcome labels in A must come from experiments. Formula counts and structural fingerprints are derived from structures, not toxicity experiments. Calculated logP and external predicted properties must not be called measured inputs. If the professor requires both X and y to be experimental, make a stricter measured-property subset and report its coverage separately. Record feature provenance in either case.

BMD/BMDL are model-derived from experimental dose-response data, not independent QSAR predictions. Retain them as a distinct evidence type. ToxCast/Tox21 are experimental bioassays with processing/model-fit steps; a model-derived hit call is not automatically an in-vivo toxicity measurement. CompTox and PubChem contain mixed evidence; classify individual records, not entire websites.

Units and endpoint matching

Check whether a prediction is linear, logarithmic, negative-log molar or another scale before conversion. The current cached TEST toxicity units are mol/kg and mol/L, with small positive linear-looking values; retain raw metadata and verify semantics from the provider. Never infer a transform from a plausible range alone. For confirmed linear values and the correct queried chemical form:

  • mg/kg = mol/kg × molecular weight(g/mol) ×1,000.
  • mg/L = mol/L × molecular weight(g/mol) ×1,000.
  • If supplied as pX = −log10(mol/unit), first recover mol/unit =10^(−pX).

Do not apply the parent acid's molecular weight to a salt or formulated mixture. Do not treat mg/kg feed or mg/kg-day as an acute mg/kg body-weight dose. Do not silently mix acid-equivalent and material-tested doses. Retain inequality/range bounds and uncertainty separately; confidence intervals are not censoring bounds.

Rat oral LD50 is a practical first matching target. Fish TEST predictions name fathead minnow, whereas the reviewed aquatic policy names rainbow trout. They are not matched-species validation pairs. The cached Daphnia prediction is labeled LC50, while the measured core is immobilization EC50. Check the model training endpoint definition and effect, not just the species and48h label. Start with matched subsets; show broader harmonization only as an explicit sensitivity with its remaining differences.

7. Model each pathway separately

Write the protocol before new scores: target, chemical group meaning, endpoint policy, exclusions, threshold/bin rules, feature set, splits, profiles, baselines, metrics, uncertainty and selection rules. Freeze the inputs and keep test results out of threshold or feature selection.

A: learn from experimental outcomes

Use measured outcomes only. Begin with descriptive distributions and study-level strata, then regression on a documented log-dose scale and/or classification when support permits. State that lower LD50 means greater acute hazard. Call the existing bins low/medium/high LD50, not low/medium/high safety. Their cutoffs are historical cohort tertiles; do not call them regulatory categories.

Use fixed common cutoffs for direct A/B bin comparison; if estimating new cutoffs, fit them on training data only and report a separate analysis. Do not turn lower bounds into exact values. Use censored methods or bound-compatible sensitivity analyses when appropriate. A sparse policy may validly return unsupported status.

B: analyze external estimates and, where useful, learn a surrogate

First compare external predictions directly across chemical groups and model families. Preserve AD Inside/Outside/unknown strata. Choose a documented primary model/version before seeing favorable results; retain others as separate sensitivity estimates. Several related models predicting one compound are not several independent compounds or votes.

If fitting our own model to B outcomes, say explicitly: the target is a model estimate. Its F1 or MAE measures reproduction of the teacher model, not agreement with real animals or plants. Keep that surrogate separate from A models. Do not use the same model's toxicity output or an algebraic equivalent as both feature and target. Predicted physicochemical features need provenance and a separate feature-block sensitivity; check for shared teacher/training-data leakage.

Shared evaluation requirements

  • Stable compound grouping across duplicate records, stereo/form-related structures where justified, and held-out scaffold/publication/provider checks. Keep all records for a chemical in one outer partition. No random row split across repeated doses, studies or predictions of the same chemical.
  • Preprocessing and hyperparameter selection use training data only. Inspect actual train/test class and scaffold support. Do not relax validation to claim a strong model; separately named easier sensitivity tests may be informative.
  • Fixed simple baselines first: majority classification; training-mean/median or simple descriptor regression. Logistic regression and random forest are useful comparisons, not presumed winners.
  • Classification: F1 (positive class defined), macro-F1 for three bins, precision, recall, balanced accuracy, AUROC where defined, confusion counts and majority baseline. Explain class imbalance and calibration where support permits.
  • Regression: MAE/RMSE on the stated transformed scale, rank correlation, residual/bias plots and prediction-interval coverage where available. Convert back carefully; a log-unit error is a multiplicative dose error.
  • Explain standardized logistic feature weights without causal claims. Forest OOB permutation importance includes uncertainty/stability, but repeat SD is not a p-value and OOB does not hold scaffolds out. If formal significance is claimed, define a justified null/permutation scheme and account for grouping and multiple testing. Otherwise call it importance/stability, not significance.
  • Report sample counts, independent groups, excluded/unsupported cases, AD, model version, source bias and uncertainty alongside scores. Never turn unknown plant activity or source membership into tested-inactive labels.

8. Compare A and B before considering C

Freeze paired, compatible compound/endpoint records with both evidence types. Analyze coverage gained, dose/rank disagreement, error by group, AD and chemical similarity, and sensitivity to experimental quality and duplicate resolution. Do not treat B's larger coverage as evidence that its values are experimentally correct. Disagreement can reveal identity, unit, context or model-domain problems.

Audit external-model training overlap: a measured record used to build TEST or another teacher is not an independent test of that teacher. Use a truly held-out experimental subset if identifiable. If training membership cannot be established, mark independence unknown and describe the comparison as agreement, not independent validation. Provider confidence/AD Inside is not proof of accuracy.

A model trained on B and evaluated against A must retain these same independence cautions. Do not claim safety or field efficacy from a toxicity surrogate. For Track2, keep literature efficacy evidence alongside non-target hazard, without multiplying them into an unsupported universal score.

9. Only later: decide whether to try combined pathway C

This is a future decision, not authorization within this planning deliverable to run C now. First complete and document A and B collection releases, model fits or explicit unsupported statuses, and paired comparisons. Write a decision note on what combination could add, the compatible endpoint, shared data lineage, uncertainty and leakage risks. If no defensible design exists, defer C explicitly.

Possible designs to assess (do not implement all by default):

  1. Use external predictions as an additional feature to predict measured outcomes, comparing against A-only on the same untouched experimental test set.
  2. Pretrain on model estimates, then update on measured training data; keep experiments as the evaluation target and track teacher training overlap.
  3. Use source-aware weighted or hierarchical models that retain evidence-type indicators and uncertainty, rather than pretending all rows are measurements.

Never simply concatenate duplicate measured/predicted labels as independent rows. Measured and predicted values must remain distinguishable and recoverable. Weights are documented assumptions, not scientific truth. Show A-only, B-only and C on common evaluation cohorts where meaningful. Test sensitivity to AD, source weights and coverage/chemical-class shifts. Fit any learned blend/calibration inside training folds only. C earns support through measured held-out performance and uncertainty, not just a higher training score or more completed rows.

  • Independently documented A and B analysis/modeling reports with actual split membership, metrics/baselines, coefficients/OOB diagnostics and limitations.
  • A paired A/B comparison with experimental-context and teacher-training overlap audits; no overstated external validation.
  • A written go/defer/design decision for C, after the separate work above.
  • A simple learning/presentation summary with “completed”, “provisional” and “planned” labels; versioned manifests, required tests and reproducible commands.

A blocked source or unsupported model is a recorded result, not permission to invent data or hide the gap. Continue other collection sources while specific access/identity issues remain open. The goal is more usable evidence and more honest comparisons, not a quota of favorable predictions.
